Eclipses: when, why and where

 
 

Eclipses happen only when a new or full Moon falls near one of the two nodes, the points where her orbit crosses the Sun's path. The Sun passes near a node twice a year. Here you see every new and full Moon of a year, which of them become eclipses and where the shadow falls.

Two animations: Run through the year (top) moves the Sun round the year circle and selects each eclipse it passes by itself; Animate the shadow (on the shadow dials) plays the eight hours around the maximum of the selected eclipse, so it applies once you have selected an eclipse (from the table, from a dial or by running through the year).

Click an eclipse (the circle with a coloured outline: orange for the Sun, blue for the Moon) to select it; you can also choose it from the list under the dial or from the table.

What the shadow looks like

Solar eclipses

New moons only. A solar eclipse happens when the new Moon passes between the Earth and the Sun, inside the coloured window. Click an eclipse (the circle with a coloured outline: orange for the Sun, blue for the Moon) to select it; you can also choose it from the list under the dial or from the table.

Lunar eclipses

Full moons only. A lunar eclipse happens when the full Moon enters the Earth's shadow, inside the coloured window. Click an eclipse (the circle with a coloured outline: orange for the Sun, blue for the Moon) to select it; you can also choose it from the list under the dial or from the table.

The shadow on the Earth's plane

We look from the Sun towards the Earth. The large circle is the Earth, the dashed line is the axis of the Moon's shadow passing over it, the orange circle is the penumbra and the filled dot is the umbra (the empty circle, the antumbra, in an annular eclipse). If the axis passes through the Earth's circle the eclipse is total or annular; if it passes beside it but the penumbra touches it, the eclipse is partial. The distance γ says how far from the centre the axis passes. For a lunar eclipse the dial shows the Earth's shadow (at the Moon's distance): the reddish umbra inside, the penumbra around it, and the Moon crossing it from west to east.

What it looks like from the place of maximum

The sky from the place where the eclipse is greatest (the point hit by the axis; for partial eclipses, the point nearest to the axis), with North up and East on the left. The orange disc is the Sun, the black one the Moon. The slider shows the Moon sliding over the Sun, from four hours before to four hours after maximum. We ignore the horizon: if the Sun is below it, it is drawn faded. For a lunar eclipse you see the Moon's disc and how much of it enters the shadow.

γ (gamma) is the distance of the shadow axis from the centre of the Earth, in Earth radii: below about 0.997 the axis hits the Earth (total or annular eclipse), up to about 1.55 only the penumbra does (partial). For a lunar eclipse the magnitude is how much of the Moon's disc enters the umbra.

How to use the dials

Eclipse season

The circle is the year, with the solar angle as on the other dials (0° at the December solstice, at the top). The coloured zone is the “eclipse season”: while the Sun is within about 17° of a node. The inner ring holds the new moons, the outer ring the full moons; those with a thick outline are eclipses. Tap one to select it.

The outer circle carries the units of the calendar you chose: months (long marks, numbered), weeks (medium marks) and, when the dial is large enough, days (fine marks). In SI these are the 12 months, and weeks start on Monday; in HPT they are 13 months of 28 days, each with exactly 4 weeks (the Year Day belongs to no week).

  1. Choose the year and the time system. The dates in the table, the summary and below the dials follow the system you chose.
  2. On the left dial you see the two coloured windows of the year. Each holds one, two or rarely three eclipses, because a new and a full Moon are about 15 days apart and a window lasts about 34 days.
  3. Tap an eclipse on the dial or a row in the table. The right dial shows where the shadow falls at maximum.
  4. Press Run through the year: the Sun's line sweeps the circle, the date appears in the centre and the Moon's phase below it changes. When the line reaches an eclipse, that eclipse is selected and the right-hand dial shows it. You can change the speed at any time.

What the shadow looks like

The Earth clock at the moment of greatest eclipse of the selected event. For a solar eclipse the line is the path of the Moon's shadow axis (four hours before and after) and the grey disc is the penumbra. For a lunar eclipse the dotted circle shows where the Moon is above the horizon. The new hours on the rim are those of the moment of maximum.

  1. For a solar eclipse the line runs from west to east across the Earth: the Moon moves east faster than the Earth's rotating shadow does.
  2. Compare the local new hour at the moment of maximum with the hour on the rim. In HPT the local hour is that of the place's 10° zone.
  3. A lunar eclipse is seen from the whole hemisphere where the Moon is above the horizon, hence the large dotted circle. A solar eclipse is seen only from a narrow strip (total, annular) or a wide area (partial).
  4. Drag the slider under the Earth clock or press Animate the shadow: you see the Moon's shadow run across the Earth, from four hours before to four hours after maximum, with the new hours on the rim changing along with it.

Why total or annular?

The Earth–Moon distance varies between about 356,000 and 407,000 km, so the Moon's apparent diameter varies between 0.49° and 0.56°, and the Sun's between 0.52° and 0.54°. If the Moon looks larger than the Sun the eclipse is total; if smaller, a ring of Sun remains around it: an annular eclipse. The ratio in the table is the Moon's apparent diameter divided by the Sun's: above 1 means total, below 1 annular.

Limits

  • We compute with the abridged Meeus series (the same as in the rest of the site). The moments of maximum are good to a minute or two, and the type, in borderline cases (hybrid eclipse, very faint penumbral), may differ from the NASA catalogue, which uses the full series.
  • We do not draw the totality strip with its exact width, only the shadow axis and the penumbra approximated as a circle at maximum.
