The planets on the zodiac: their path across the sky

A horoscope does not read the zodiac itself; it reads where the Sun, the Moon and the planets stand on its map at a given moment. The dials below show exactly that, for any date: the position of each body in the 12 signs, its path through time (with retrogrades) and the angles between bodies, which astrologers call “aspects”. The positions are computed, not told; interpretations are not part of the dials (see the tutorial “Astrology in brief”).

 

Speeds are in simulated days per real second. The dials are oriented like the rest of the system (December solstice at the top); zodiac signs are tropical, with 0° Aries at the March equinox. Speeds are simulated time per real second. In the International System (SI), the simulation advances in standard minutes and hours; in Human precessional time (HPT), in new minutes and hours (1 new hour = 40 SI min). The day has the same length in both systems, so speeds in days or years are identical.

How to use the dials

Dial 1. The zodiac and the planets

The outer ring holds the 12 tropical signs, 30° each. Under each sign glyph is the glyph of its traditional ruling planet (Aries: Mars, Taurus: Venus, Gemini and Virgo: Mercury, Cancer: the Moon, Leo: the Sun, Libra: Venus, Scorpio: Mars, Sagittarius and Pisces: Jupiter, Capricorn and Aquarius: Saturn). Inside, each body has its own circle: the Sun outermost, Pluto innermost (Chiron sits between Saturn and Uranus). A circle with a dashed edge means retrograde motion. The outer ring holds the 13 real constellations at their true sizes (Ophiuchus is darker): it is not fixed, but turns slowly against the signs with precession. The orange triangle is the vernal point (0° tropical).

  1. Press Now and read the table: which sign each body is in, which constellation it is actually in (sometimes not the sign’s namesake), and whether it moves direct or retrograde.
  2. Start the animation at 10 days per second. The Moon races round its circle, the Sun makes one lap a year, Mercury and Venus keep it company, and Jupiter and Saturn seem still.
  3. Switch to 1 year per second and count: Jupiter changes sign about once a year, Saturn every two and a half years.
  4. Choose 1,000 years per second and watch the constellation ring: it turns against the signs, about 1° every 72 years. Press Vernal point enters Pisces (year 100), then compare with “Now”: the vernal point’s constellation has changed, the tropical sign Aries has not.

Dial 2. Angles between bodies

Bodies are placed on the circle at their longitude. A line appears when the angular distance between two bodies is, within the chosen orb, 0° (conjunction), 60° (sextile), 90° (square), 120° (trine) or 180° (opposition). The table gives the exact angle and how far it is from the perfect one.

  1. Change the orb from 3° to 8°: the larger the tolerance, the more “aspects” appear. The orb is not a physical quantity but a convention that differs from one astrologer to another.
  2. Start the animation at 1 day per second and watch how long an angle lasts: the Moon forms and dissolves angles within hours, the slow planets within months.
  3. A question to think about: with an 8° orb, how much of the year passes with no angle between planets? (Try it and see.)

Dial 3. The path through time

The horizontal axis is time, the vertical axis ecliptic longitude, split into signs. The line does not always climb: every few months a planet stops, goes back (retrogrades), stops again and resumes. The orange dot is the chosen moment.

  1. Pick Mars and a 2-year window: the retrograde loop shows as a dent in the line.
  2. Press Next retrograde: the dial jumps to the exact moment the planet reverses (the station).
  3. Read the sign changes list: a planet can enter a sign, leave it (arrow ←) and re-enter. That is why “time in a sign” in chapter 11 of the book is an average.
  4. Under “Time-axis divisions” choose the month system: 12 SI months (the usual calendar) or 13 HPT months of 28 days. The thick vertical lines are the year boundaries; months appear when the window is 4 years or less (and only for the Sun, the Moon and the inner planets). Under “Vertical axis” choose between the 12 zodiac signs (equal, 30° each) and the 13 IAU constellations (unequal, slowly shifting with precession); “Show names” adds the full name.
  5. Under “Positions relative to the Sun” choose conjunctions and oppositions: the symbols ☌ (conjunction: the planet is in the same direction as the Sun), ☍ (opposition: in the opposite direction) and, optionally, □ (square, 90° and 270°) appear on the path, like the marks of Dial 4. Retrograde motion is always found around an opposition (outer planets) or an inferior conjunction (Mercury and Venus, when they pass between the Earth and the Sun).

Dial 4. The circular path

The same path as in Dial 3, wrapped around a circle. The centre is the start of the time window and the rim is its end: the coloured line spirals outwards, and a retrograde loop shows up as a small knot on the spiral. The black dot is the chosen moment. With Against signs / constellations the angle is ecliptic longitude, read on the outer ring (signs or constellations, following the “Vertical axis” choice of Dial 3); the Sun is the yellow dot. With Against the Sun the Sun stays fixed at the top and the angle is the elongation: 0° conjunction, 90° square, 180° opposition.

  1. Pick Mars, the 10 years window and Against signs / constellations: the spiral makes almost five turns, about one every two years.
  2. Switch to Against the Sun: Venus never gets further than about 47° from the Sun, so its spiral swings around the top of the dial. Mars goes round the whole circle, passing through opposition.
  3. Pick the Moon and the 6 months window: the six turns against the Sun are the lunations. 180° is the full Moon, 0° is the new Moon.

Dial 5. Why planets seem to go retrograde

A view from above, from outside the solar system. The Sun is at the centre, and the Earth and the chosen planet both move in the same direction, always. From the Earth the planet is seen against the background stars, on the outer ring (the same signs as in the other dials). The thin lines are the directions in which we see it at 26 moments around the chosen one, and where they end on the ring forms its apparent path (the later the moment, the further out). The dotted stretch is the retrograde motion: the planet seems to go backwards although it never changed direction on its orbit. The cause is overtaking: when one of the two planets catches up with the other, the line of sight turns the other way for a while, just as when you pass a car and it seems to move backwards against the distant trees. For an outer planet it is the Earth that overtakes; for Mercury and Venus, they overtake the Earth.

  1. Pick Mars, press Next retrograde and start the animation at 1 day per second. Watch the thin lines: they advance along the ring, turn back while the Earth overtakes Mars, then resume.
  2. Pick Venus: here it is Venus that overtakes the Earth. The retrograde motion appears when it passes between the Earth and the Sun (inferior conjunction), and the line of sight turns fastest.
  3. Pick Jupiter or Saturn: the Earth's orbit becomes small in the drawing, but the effect is the same. Retrograde motion lasts months and repeats every year, because the Earth overtakes them every year.
  4. Pick the Moon: nothing shows. We do not overtake the Moon, because it orbits the Earth, not the Sun.

What these dials do not show

  • The Sun and Moon positions come from Meeus’s formulas, as in the rest of the system. Planet positions come from E. M. Standish’s orbital elements (JPL, Table 1, valid mainly for 1800–2050). Compared with an independent astronomy library (ephem), Mercury–Saturn longitudes differ by less than 0.25° between 1900 and 2200 (our own check, on a handful of dates).
  • Chiron is a small body with a very elongated orbit, perturbed by Jupiter and Saturn. Its path is computed from NASA’s 1996 osculating elements without perturbations, so it is approximate: precision drops with the decades away from 1996, from a few tenths of a degree to a few degrees. It is not among the traditional ruling planets.
  • Signs are tropical (tied to the March equinox). The constellation in the table is computed by longitude only, with IAU boundaries, as on the Sun and Moon page.
  • The dials show positions and angles, that is geometry. They do not say, and cannot say, what will happen to a person.
