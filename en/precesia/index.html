What precession is, in plain words

The Earth spins around its axis, but the axis itself turns very slowly, like a spinning top. This motion takes about 25,772 years and is called the precession of the equinoxes. Move the slider and see what changes in the sky.

A whole cycle lasts 25,772 years; at 2,000 years per second you see it in about 13 seconds.

Move the slider below. The left circle shows where the Earth's axis points in the chosen year. The right circle shows which era we are in.

  1. The spinning top. A spinning top keeps its axis tilted, but the axis slowly draws a cone. The Earth's axis, tilted 23.4° to its orbit, does the same, in a cycle of 25,772 years.
  2. The pole star changes. The axis points to a different spot in the sky in each epoch. Today it points to Polaris, 4,800 years ago it pointed to Thuban, and in about 11,700 years it will point to Vega.
  3. The equinox slides across the sky. The point where the Sun stands at the March equinox moves through the constellations by about 1° every 71.6 years (50.3 arcseconds per year), against the Sun's own motion. That is where the popular “Age of Pisces” and “Age of Aquarius” come from.
  4. An era is a 30° sector. We divide the 360° circle into 12 equal sectors, one per zodiac constellation. A sector takes about 2,147 years to cross. In the system these are the 12 precessional eras (ER 1–12). They are units of time, not astrology.
  5. How to read ER and AE. ER is the era and AE is the year within the era (no year zero). The year 2026 CE is ER 12 (Pisces), AE 1734, and 785 BCE is ER 11, AE 1072. ER 1 (Aquarius) begins in the year 2441. The reference star is Spica.
  6. Limits. Real precession accelerates slightly, so the real era boundaries are not equally spaced. The system's equal eras differ from them by 6.5 years at the start of ER 12 and by 33 years at ER 11, and by more further back. An ER-AE code is unique only within one 25,772-year cycle.

Why it matters for the calendar

Because precession is the only celestial motion slow and regular enough to measure all of history with a single cycle, without other conventions. The hour comes from the Earth's rotation, the year from its orbit around the Sun, the month from the Moon's orbit, and the era from the wobble of the axis. All four are physical phenomena that anyone can observe.
