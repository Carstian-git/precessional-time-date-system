The seasons: the year wheel

 
 

The seasons do not come from the Earth getting nearer to or farther from the Sun, but from the 23.4° tilt of its axis. At two of the year's four turning points, the solstices, one hemisphere leans most toward the Sun; at the other two, the equinoxes, the Sun stands exactly over the equator and day equals night. Here you see them on the year wheel, in both time systems.

The year wheel

On the wheel the year starts at the top, at the December solstice, as in the human precessional time. The outer rings are months, weeks and, if there is room, days. The ☉ marker is the Sun on the chosen day.

The Sun's declination and the day length

The analemma

What the analemma is

The analemma is the figure-eight the Sun traces in the sky if you photograph it every day at the same clock time. The vertical axis is the declination (the seasons), the horizontal axis is the equation of time: how many minutes true solar noon departs from the clock's mean noon. The dot is the chosen day, the circles are the year's four turning points.

Our clock measures an imaginary Sun moving uniformly. The real Sun departs from it for two reasons: the Earth's orbit is elliptical, so its speed varies, and the tilted axis makes the ecliptic project onto the equator at unequal rates. Together they give a departure of up to 16 minutes 25 seconds in early November and −14 minutes 15 seconds in mid-February. To find true noon at your place, enter the longitude.

How to use the dials

  1. Choose the year, the time system and the hemisphere; the season names swap in the southern hemisphere.
  2. Drag the “Day of the year” slider or press “Run through the year”: the Sun moves on the wheel and the chart shows the declination and day length of that same day.
  3. Change the latitude: at 0° the day is always 12 hours (18 new hours), and beyond the polar circles polar night and polar day appear.

Why the seasons are not equal

The Earth's orbit is an ellipse, and it moves faster when nearer the Sun, in early January. So in the northern hemisphere astronomical summer is a few days longer than winter. The table gives each season's length in days and in HPT weeks.

What the page does not show

  • Sunrise and sunset also depend on terrain and refraction; here we use the standard definition, with the Sun at −0.833° below the horizon.
  • Meteorological seasons (December–February, etc.) are calendar conventions and do not match the astronomical ones.
  • Day length is computed for 12:00 UTC of each day; the change within a day is negligible.
