The Sun and the Moon: who catches up with whom

 

Speeds are simulated time per real second (the second of the International System, SI, on your clock). In SI the simulation advances in standard minutes and hours; in Human precessional time (HPT), in new minutes and hours: 1 new hour = 40 SI min, 1 new minute = 4 SI min. The day has the same length in both systems (36 × 40 min = 24 h), so speeds in days are identical.

The Sun and the Moon both move eastward among the stars, but not at the same speed: the Moon is about 13 times faster. The dials below use the same angles as the clock and calendar of the human precessional system (the solar angle, the lunar angle) and show, in turn, the race between the two bodies, the nodes of the Moon's orbit, the Moon's shadow on the Earth clock with its new 10° time zones, and the eclipses of a year. The three dials sit side by side so you can follow them at once. This page accompanies chapter 12 of the book “O lume contra timp” (in Romanian).

How to use the dials

Dial 1. The race: the Moon chases the Sun

The circle is the ecliptic, the Sun's path among the stars, with 0° at the December solstice as in the calendar. The Sun (orange) and the Moon (blue) move clockwise; the blue arc between them is the lunar angle, the Moon's distance from the Sun, and the phase is shown in the centre.

  1. Press Play at 1 day per second and watch the blue hand: it makes a full turn in about 27 seconds while the orange hand barely moves.
  2. Press Next new moon. The two hands overlap and the lunar angle is 0°. Then press Next full moon: the hands are opposite, at 180°.
  3. Read the “behind” line: relative to the Sun, the Moon falls behind by 12.19° a day on average, which is 1.22 new hours or about 49 standard minutes. That is why it rises, on average, almost an hour later each day (by our calculations).
  4. Compare the speeds: the Sun between 0.95° and 1.02° a day, the Moon between 11.8° and 15.3° a day. They are not constant, because orbits are not circles.

Dial 2. The nodes: why there is not an eclipse every month

The same circle with the nodes of the Moon's orbit: ☊ (ascending node) and ☋ (descending node). The orange zones next to the nodes are the “eclipse seasons” (the Sun within about 17° of a node).

Side view: the straight line is the ecliptic, the blue curve the Moon's orbit, tilted by 5.145°. The orange band is the latitude below which a new moon can bring a solar eclipse, according to the spreadsheet (1.58°). The vertical scale is enlarged relative to the horizontal one.

  1. Start the animation at 5 days per second. Usually the Moon passes above or below the Sun, outside the orange band: no eclipse.
  2. Press Next new moon several times and watch the Moon's latitude. An eclipse becomes possible only when the latitude is small.
  3. Press the moment buttons: 12.08.2026 or 02.08.2027. The Sun is then in an eclipse season, with the Moon near a node.
  4. Notice that the nodes do not stand still: they move westward by about 19.3° a year (one turn in 18.6 years), which is why eclipse seasons come about 19 days earlier each year.

Dial 3. The Earth clock: where the shadow falls

The same Earth clock as in the app and in chapter 16 of the book: the Earth seen from above the North Pole, with the 36 new time zones of 10°, each with its new hour on the outer ring. The Sun (orange) is above the zone where it is noon, hour 18. The small moon shows the point beneath it, with its real phase. At a new moon the Moon's shadow appears: the dark circle is the penumbra and the dot in the centre is the shadow axis. At a full moon, the dotted circle shows the half of the globe from which the Moon is visible.

  1. Press Total solar eclipse, 12.08.2026. The shadow falls near zone F−03, in the North Atlantic north-west of Iceland, where the new clock reads about hour 23.
  2. Start the animation at 1 minute per second: the shadow crosses the Earth from zone to zone, and the hours on the outer ring change with it. The other two dials move at the same time.
  3. Go to 03.03.2026 (total lunar eclipse): the dotted circle shows where the Moon is above the horizon, nearly half of the Earth.
  4. Compare 17.02.2026 and 02.08.2027: the first is annular, the second total. The type depends on the Moon's distance at that moment, not on the date.

Dial 4. The eclipses of a year: “possible” and “real”

For each new and full moon of the chosen year, the table shows the Moon's latitude and whether the “Calendar an” sheet of the Excel workbook would write “possible eclipse”. For 2026–2028 the real eclipse is added, from NASA and EclipseWise (Table 11.2 in the book). Click a row to put it on the dials.

  1. Choose 2026, 2027 and 2028 and compare the columns: almost every month marked “possible” also has a real eclipse, but not all of them.
  2. Try a year of the 20th century. Without a real-eclipse column you will only see the mark. Over the whole century the sheet marks 244 solar eclipses against 228 in the NASA catalogue (by our calculations).
  3. Notice that penumbral lunar eclipses (three in 2027) are not marked: the sheet marks only umbral ones.

What these dials do not show

  • The outer ring shows the path of the Sun and the Moon through the present-day constellations on the ecliptic, with the boundaries fixed by the International Astronomical Union (13, not 12: Ophiuchus, highlighted, holds the Sun for about 18 days). The graduations of the inner circle, the solar angle from the December solstice, are unchanged. Constellation boundaries are rounded values (about 0.1°) and drift slowly through precession (by our calculations, about 0.014° a year).
  • Positions are computed with Meeus formulas, in JavaScript, for the centre of the Earth; latitudes may differ by a few hundredths of a degree from the calculation with the ephem library used in the book.
  • The node is the mean node; the true node oscillates by up to 1.5° around it.
  • The dials do not predict eclipses and do not say where they will be seen. “Possible” only means that the Moon's latitude at the phase is below a threshold. Predicting a specific eclipse, with its shadow on the ground, takes much more (distances, radii, ΔT) and is done with the NASA catalogues.
  • Times are in UTC, not local time.
