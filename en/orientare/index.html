Direction and position with a clock

A clock can tell you where you are in two ways: which direction north lies and how many degrees of longitude you are at. Both rest on the same fact, that the Earth makes one turn in 24 standard hours, that is 36 new hours. This page summarises what the book explains.

How do you find north with a clock?

With a classic 12-hour wristwatch you hold the dial flat and aim the hour hand at the Sun. In the northern hemisphere, south lies halfway between the hour hand and 12; in the southern one, the 12 is aimed at the Sun and north lies halfway. In daylight saving time you use 1 instead of 12. The method is described in the US Army land navigation manual (FM 3-25.26, §9-5b), which warns that it can be wrong, especially at low latitudes.

The halving comes from the fact that the hour hand of a 12-hour watch turns twice a day while the Sun turns once. The 36-new-hour dial makes a single turn a day, like the Sun, and noon is at 18. Held flat with the hour hand aimed at the Sun, its mark 0 points north and its mark 18 south (in the northern temperate zone; in the southern one the roles are reversed). There is nothing left to halve, and with local solar time daylight saving time disappears too.

The direction stays approximate: the error grows toward low latitudes, and the equation of time shifts the Sun by up to about 16 standard minutes, that is 4°, from mean time. The 18-hour dial turns twice a day and needs the same halving as the 12-hour watch. There is no HPT wristwatch yet; the Earth clock in the app shows the same thing: the hour hand follows the Sun.

How do you find longitude from a time difference?

The Earth turns 1° in exactly 240 seconds, that is one new minute. A new hour is therefore 10° of longitude, a new minute 1°, a new second 0.1°. You need a clock that keeps the reference time of zone 0 (Greenwich), and the moment of local noon, that is the shortest shadow. You read the clock at that moment. The difference between hour 18 and the time read turns directly into degrees: hours × 10 + minutes + seconds ÷ 10.

Example. The reference clock reads 14 hours, 6 minutes and 0 seconds at local noon. The difference up to hour 18 is 3 hours and 4 minutes, that is 3 × 10° + 4 × 1° = 34° east.

An error of one new minute in the reference clock means 1° of longitude, about 111 km at the equator; one new second, about 11 km; one subsecond, about 460 metres (by our calculations). The equation of time, which shifts true noon by up to ±4°, must be corrected from tables.

What the clock does not show

The clock does not give latitude. For that you need the Sun's height at noon or the Pole Star, which do not depend on how we count time. Nor does the app's “local” mode work backwards: it starts from a chosen place and shows the time, not the position.

What remains is a gain in coherence, not a promise of a ready-made instrument: with an HPT clock, direction and longitude read more directly than with the classic one, with the same limits of accuracy.
